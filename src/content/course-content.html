Chapter 1 · §1

Can a machine think? Turing's answer

Every introduction to artificial intelligence begins with the same awkward question, and most of them get stuck on it. Alan Turing's contribution, in 1950, was to replace the question with one that could actually be answered.

Pause and thinkSuppose someone claimed to have built a thinking machine. What test would satisfy you? What would you need to see — and what, honestly, would you be unable to see however hard you looked?

Turing's proposal is disarmingly simple. A judge sits at a terminal and holds two conversations by text: one with a hidden person, one with a hidden machine. The judge may ask anything. Afterwards the judge must say which is which. If, over many such trials, the machine cannot be reliably told from the person, Turing suggested we should stop arguing about whether it "really" thinks and simply grant that it does.

Notice what the test refuses to do. It does not open the machine up. It does not ask how the answers are produced. It judges behaviour alone — and it insists on text precisely so that a voice, a face or a body cannot settle the matter before the conversation has begun.

Why it is not enough

You may have found the experiment easy; the software gave itself away by being too tidy. But imagine a version that did not. Would passing the test show that it understood you? Here is the difficulty. A candidate can memorise interview answers and get the job without understanding it. A language model can learn what a natural reply looks like without being able to reason reliably when the situation changes. Producing a convincing answer and understanding an answer are different things, and the test cannot tell them apart.

Two further objections are worth having ready. The test lives entirely in text, so it says nothing about perception or physical skill: a system could pass and still be unable to find its way across a room. And the verdict depends on who is judging and what they thought to ask; a credulous judge with dull questions makes the test trivial.

The second objection led to a stronger proposal, the Total Turing Test, in which the machine must also see (through cameras), recognise what is around it, and act on objects (with a robot arm). A chatbot that explains how to replace a failed cooling fan is one thing; a system that must locate the fan, avoid the hazards beside it and carry out the replacement is quite another. The total test asks for both.

Key ideaFluency is evidence of fluency. It is not evidence of understanding, and it is not evidence of correctness. Keep this in your pocket; it returns in Chapter 6, §2, as the single most important habit for anyone using an AI assistant at work.
Chapter 1 · §2

Describing the job before building the worker

We say "intelligent agent" as though the phrase were self-explanatory. It is not, and a surprising amount of muddle disappears once you describe an agent's job with four plain questions.

Pause and thinkYou are asked to build a robot that plays football. Before you touch a single motor: how will you know whether it is playing well? What is around it? What can it do? What can it sense?

Those four questions have a name — PEAS, for Performance, Environment, Actuators, Sensors — and the order matters. Performance first: what counts as success? For the footballer, scoring, winning, and staying within the rules. Environment next: the pitch, the ball, team-mates, opponents, a referee. Actuators: the means of acting on that world — motors, legs, a kicking mechanism. Sensors: the means of perceiving it — cameras, distance and motion sensors.

Why this order? Because a system cannot choose a sensible action if the information it needs is not available, nor carry out the action if it has no means to. Deciding what "good" means before you decide what to measure is the discipline that keeps projects honest.

Chapter 1 · §3

What makes a problem hard: eight properties of an environment

Two tasks can look alike and be utterly different in difficulty. Chess and driving both involve choosing moves; one has been mastered by machines for decades and the other has not. The difference lies in the environment.

Pause and thinkThink about a robot vacuum cleaner. Can it see the whole flat at once? Does the world stand still while it decides? If it tries to move forward, does it always end up where it intended? Each of those questions is one of the properties below.

The textbook list has eight pairs. An environment is fully or partially observable: can the agent see everything that matters, or must it remember and infer? It involves a single agent or several, and if several, they may be competitive or co-operative. It is deterministic or not: does the same action from the same state always produce the same result? It is episodic or sequential: does a decision now change what is possible later? It is static or dynamic: does the world change while the agent is thinking? Its states and actions are discrete or continuous. And its rules are known or unknown to the agent in advance.

For the vacuum cleaner most of these fall on the harder side. It cannot see every room or the sock under the sofa, so it is partially observable. People and pets wander through, so it is dynamic. Wheels slip and carpets vary, so in practice it is non-deterministic. Entering a narrow corridor now affects what can be cleaned next, so it is sequential. Its position is a continuous quantity, though a designer may choose to model it as a grid of cells. The layout is unknown until explored. It is usually a single agent, so competition does not arise — unless a second robot shares the flat, in which case the two may co-operate.

A caution about the labels. They describe your model of the task as much as the task itself. A data-centre controller with incomplete temperature readings, unpredictable fan failures and changing power conditions is partially observable, non-deterministic and dynamic — but a simplified model might reasonably treat it otherwise. The point of the list is not to pin the right badge on each problem; it is to make you notice, before you build, which of these difficulties you are taking on.

Key ideaPartially observable, dynamic, sequential, non-deterministic, continuous, unknown: each of these makes a problem harder, and most real problems have several of them. Know which you face.

Chapter 2 · §1

Two ways of deciding: reflex and purpose

Every agent runs the same loop — sense, decide, act — and the whole of the subject lies in the middle word. The simplest agents decide by reflex; the interesting ones decide by looking ahead.

Pause and thinkA thermostat switches the heating on when the room is cold. A chess player considers what the opponent will do after each candidate move. Both are deciding. What, exactly, is the difference?

The thermostat is a simple reflex agent. It looks at the current reading, matches it against a rule — if temperature below 18 °C then heat — and acts. It has no memory, no notion of what will follow, and no alternatives to weigh. That is also its virtue: it is fast, cheap and easy to trust for a condition that is obvious. Its weakness appears the moment the right action depends on something it cannot currently see, or on what will happen next. A rule that says "if a miner's temperature exceeds 85 °C, raise an alert" is fine; it is no help in choosing which of several remedies to apply.

The chess player is a goal-based agent. It keeps an internal picture of the situation, it knows what outcome it wants, and it searches among sequences of actions for one that leads there. That buys flexibility — the same agent can pursue a different goal tomorrow — and the ability to foresee consequences. The price is a model of how the world responds to actions, and the computation to search through it.

People often assume that adding learning turns a reflex agent into a thoughtful one. It does not. A reflex agent can learn better thresholds or better rules from feedback; a goal-based agent can learn a more accurate model of the world, or faster ways to search it. Learning improves whatever kind of agent you have. The kind is fixed by how it decides.

Key ideaA reflex agent maps the present percept straight to an action. A goal-based agent asks what it wants and searches for a route there. Learning can sharpen either; it does not change which one you have.
Chapter 2 · §2

Turning a puzzle into something a computer can search

"Search for a route to the goal" is a fine phrase, but a computer cannot search a wooden puzzle. It can only search a precise description of one. Writing that description is the first real skill of the subject, and the 8-puzzle is the classic place to learn it.

You have probably held one: eight numbered tiles in a three-by-three frame with one gap, the tiles sliding into the gap. Let us describe it so that a program could work with it. We need to say what a state is, which state we start in, which we want, what actions are available, and — this turns out to matter — how we might guess how far we are from the goal.

Pause and thinkWhat is the least information you need to record about the puzzle to know exactly which moves are possible? What can you safely throw away?

A state is just the positions of the tiles. A three-by-three array of integers does it, with 0 standing for the gap: the position in the experiment below is [[7,2,4],[5,0,6],[8,3,1]]. Everything else — the colour of the tiles, their weight, the material of the frame — is irrelevant to which moves are legal, so we leave it out. This leaving-out is called abstraction, and it is not laziness: every detail you keep is a detail the search must carry.

The goal state is the arrangement we want, here [[0,1,2],[3,4,5],[6,7,8]]. An action is sliding a neighbouring tile into the gap; it is easier to think of it as moving the gap up, down, left or right, within the frame. Each action produces a new state, and the states joined by actions form a graph that a program can explore.

How big is the problem?

Nine squares and nine distinct things to put in them (counting the gap) gives 9 × 8 × 7 × … × 1 = 9! = 362,880 arrangements. That is an over-count. Sliding tiles preserves a parity property, so from any particular start only half of the arrangements — 181,440 — can ever be reached. The number matters not because a search will visit them all, but because it tells you how much a good search must avoid.

A sense of direction

Searching without guidance is like trying every road out of a town until one reaches the sea. A heuristic is a quick estimate of how far the goal is, used to try promising roads first. The simplest for this puzzle counts the tiles that are not yet where they belong; at the start that is 8, at the goal 0. A better one, the Manhattan distance, adds up for each tile how many squares across and down it must still travel. Neither is the true distance — they are estimates — but both are zero exactly at the goal, and both never over-estimate, which is what allows the A* algorithm in the experiment to find a solution it can prove is shortest.

Chapter 2 · §3

Getting out of a hole: simulated annealing

Many practical problems — timetabling, routing, laying out a factory — have far too many states to search exhaustively, and no tidy heuristic. For these we borrow a trick from the blacksmith.

Pause and thinkYou are walking in fog over hilly ground, trying to reach the lowest point. Your rule is simple: always step downhill. What goes wrong?

What goes wrong is that you stop in the first hollow you find, which may be a shallow dip while a deep valley lies a few hundred yards away. Always stepping downhill is called greedy descent, and its failure has a name: a local minimum.

Now the blacksmith. When metal is heated and then cooled slowly, its atoms have time to jostle out of awkward arrangements and settle into a low-energy, orderly crystal. Cool it suddenly and it freezes into whatever disordered state it happened to be in. The slow process is called annealing, and the computational idea named after it is this: allow the search, occasionally, to step uphill. Early on, when the "temperature" is high, accept worse moves freely and explore widely. As the temperature falls, accept them more and more rarely, until the search settles.

The rule is precise. If a proposed move makes the cost worse by ΔE, accept it anyway with probability exp(−ΔE / T), where T is the current temperature. A small worsening at high temperature is nearly always accepted; a large worsening at low temperature almost never.

Chapter 3 · §1

Living with not knowing

Chapter 2 assumed the agent could see its state and predict the result of its actions. Almost nothing in the world is so obliging. This chapter is about deciding well when you cannot be sure.

Pause and thinkA sensor reads 31 °C. Is the room "hot"? A machine has run for 400 days; will it fail tomorrow? An expert says "it's probably the pump". These are three quite different kinds of not-knowing. Can you say how they differ?

The first is vagueness: "hot" has no sharp edge, and a room can be somewhat hot and somewhat warm at once. Fuzzy logic handles this by letting a statement be partly true. The second is chance: the machine either will or will not fail, and what we lack is knowledge of which. Probability theory handles this, and most of this chapter is about it. The third is confidence in a rule of thumb: the expert's "probably" is not a measured frequency but a degree of belief attached to a diagnosis, which older expert systems represented as a certainty factor. Three tools, three kinds of doubt; the skill is matching tool to doubt.

The question people get backwards

Here is a puzzle that defeats most people on first meeting, including many doctors. One email in a hundred is junk. A filter catches 90% of junk, and wrongly flags 7% of legitimate mail. The filter has just flagged a message. How likely is it that the message really is junk?

The tempting answer is "about 90%". It is wrong by a factor of eight. The 90% tells you how often the filter fires when junk is present. You were asked something different: how often junk is present when the filter fires. Those are not the same, and the gap between them depends on how rare junk is in the first place — the base rate.

Worked exampleTake 10,000 messages so the percentages become counts. One percent are junk: 100 junk, 9,900 legitimate. The filter catches 90% of the 100 junk messages — 90 of them. It also wrongly flags 7% of the 9,900 legitimate ones — 693 of them. So 783 messages are flagged altogether, and only 90 of those are junk. The probability that a flagged message is junk is 90 ÷ 783 ≈ 0.115, or about 11.5%. Written as a formula this is Bayes' theorem: P(junk | flagged) = P(flagged | junk)·P(junk) ÷ P(flagged) = (0.90 × 0.01) ÷ (0.90 × 0.01 + 0.07 × 0.99) ≈ 0.1149.

Chapter 3 · §2

Chains of chance and the value of a position

Probability so far has been about single events. Now we let time run. A system moves from state to state, each move a matter of chance, and we ask what a given state is worth.

A Markov chain is a set of states with, for each one, the probabilities of where it goes next. Its defining assumption is that the next step depends only on the present state, not on the path that led there. The figure shows a small maze: from A the walker goes to B with probability 0.8 and finishes at D with probability 0.2; from B, to C or D in the same proportions; from C, back to C or on to D. Each visit to a state also earns a reward — 1 at A, 2 at B, 3 at C, nothing at D.

What is a state worth?

Suppose you find yourself at B. You collect 2 at once. Then, with probability 0.8, you move to C — which the figure tells us is worth 8.3 in expected future reward — and with probability 0.2 you finish, worth nothing more. The expected value of where you go next is 0.8 × 8.3 + 0.2 × 0 = 6.64. One more ingredient: a reward later counts for less than the same reward now, so we multiply future value by a discount factor γ, here 0.8. The value of B is therefore 2 + 0.8 × 6.64 = 7.312.

That is the Bellman equation in miniature: the value of a state is its immediate reward plus the discounted, probability-weighted value of its successors. It is worth checking the convention: treating the 2 as B's own reward gives v(A) = 1 + 0.8 × 0.8 × 7.312 ≈ 5.68, which matches the 5.7 printed on the figure. Treating the 2 as earned only on the B→C arrow would not. When a figure is ambiguous, consistency with what it does tell you is the way to resolve it.

Chapter 3 · §3

Adding choice: Markov decision processes

In the chain above the walker merely drifts. Give the walker a choice at each state and you have a Markov decision process — the model behind reinforcement learning, and behind a great deal of what is now called AI.

An MDP has states, as before, but at each state there are actions to choose from, and each action has its own probabilities of where it leads and what it pays. A rule saying which action to take in each state is a policy. The agent's objective is not to grab the largest next reward but to choose a policy that maximises the expected total discounted reward over the whole future, E[Σ γt Rt+1]. A manager deciding whether to restart a machine now or wait for it to cool is facing exactly this trade-off: quick output against a higher chance of failure later.

A three-state decision process with actions ab, ba, bc, ca and cb. Look carefully: not every action's outcomes are fully specified.

Judging a policy, then improving it

Two operations do most of the work in this field. Policy evaluation asks how good a given policy is: for each state, average over the actions the policy takes, and for each action average over its outcomes, of reward plus discounted value of the next state. Done synchronously, every state's new value is computed from the old values and then all are replaced at once; you never use a freshly computed v(A) to work out v(B) in the same sweep. Policy improvement then asks, in each state, which single action has the highest such value — written Q(s, a) — and adopts it. Alternate the two and the policy climbs towards the best one.

A word about the figure

You may have tried to evaluate the three-state process in the figure and found you could not. That is not your failing. The arrow from A to B is marked 0.5, but where the other half of the probability goes is not stated; at C the arrows marked 0.25, 0.75 and 0.5 cannot all belong to one action. Key ideaA policy is a rule for acting; its quality is the expected discounted return it earns. Evaluate it by averaging over actions and outcomes; improve it by choosing, in each state, the action with the highest Q. And when the model is incomplete, say so.

Chapter 4 · §1

Why language is harder than it looks

We use language all day without noticing how much of it is guesswork. A machine notices, because it cannot guess the way we do.

Pause and think"I saw the man with a telescope." Who has the telescope? Read it again. Can you make it mean the other thing?

If you managed both readings, you have found a syntactic ambiguity: the words are perfectly clear, but they can be grouped in two ways. Either with a telescope describes how I saw, or it describes the man. Nothing in the sentence decides. There is a second, simpler kind. "I went to the bank" is ambiguous because one word has two meanings — a place that holds money and the side of a river. That is lexical ambiguity.

Humans resolve both without effort, using everything around the sentence: what was said before, what the speaker is likely to want, what the world is like. A system that processes language must do the same, and when the surrounding context is thin it will sometimes pick the wrong reading. Instructions at work are full of such traps. "Send the report to the manager with the updated figures" could mean the report must contain the figures, or that it goes to the manager who already has them; a careful reader asks, and a careful writer rephrases.

Chapter 4 · §2

Networks of simple units

An artificial neural network is less mysterious than its name. It is a stack of layers, each turning a list of numbers into another list of numbers by multiplying and adding. What makes it interesting is what the shape of the stack does.

Picture a layer of 1,000 units feeding a layer of 1,000 units, every unit in the first connected to every unit in the second. Each connection carries a number — a weight — and there are 1,000 × 1,000 = a million of them. Call this Network A. Now picture Network B: 1,000 units feeding a narrow layer of 100, which feeds 1,000 again. It has 1,000 × 100 + 100 × 1,000 = 200,000 weights, a fifth as many.

With fewer weights B needs less memory and fewer multiplications, and its narrow waist may help it generalise: forced to summarise 1,000 numbers in 100, it cannot simply memorise the training data, so it is less prone to overfitting. "May" is the honest word — the same squeeze can throw away something that mattered.

What B has given up is capacity. If the layers are linear — plain multiplying and adding, no curve in between — then everything B does must pass through 100 numbers, and the mapping it computes has rank at most 100. A can compute any linear mapping of rank up to 1,000. Think of a thousand sensor readings sent down a channel that carries only a hundred independent values; efficient, but detail is lost. This is a mathematical limit, not something more training will fix.

The smallest possible network

To see what one unit actually does, build the simplest function there is. NOT takes a single input x and returns its opposite: 0 becomes 1, 1 becomes 0. A unit computes a weighted sum of its inputs, adds a bias, and then applies an activation — here a step: output 1 if the sum is at least zero, 0 otherwise. For NOT we need a larger input to push the output down, so the weight must be negative. Take w = −1 and b = 0.5. For x = 0 the sum is 0.5, so the output is 1; for x = 1 the sum is −0.5, so the output is 0. Done. Equivalently, the unit fires when −x crosses a threshold of −0.5.

Chapter 4 · §3

Learning to ask the right question first

A decision tree is the most legible kind of machine learning: a sequence of questions, each chosen because it does the most to settle the matter. "Does the most" has a precise meaning, and it is the last idea in this book.

Pause and thinkSix students sat a module. Four passed. If I tell you nothing else, how uncertain are you about any one student's result? Now suppose all six had passed. How uncertain then?

That uncertainty has a measure, entropy. For two outcomes with proportions p and 1 − p, it is H = −p log₂ p − (1 − p) log₂ (1 − p), in bits. If every student passed, p = 1 and H = 0: no uncertainty at all. If half passed, H = 1 bit, the most uncertain a two-way outcome can be. For four of six, H ≈ 0.918: nearly maximal, because both results are common, but a little below 1 because passing is the likelier.

Which question reduces the uncertainty most?

Suppose we know two things about each student: their previous grade — poor, moderate or excellent — and whether they revised. Which fact is more useful for predicting a pass? Split the students by grade and look inside each group. The poor group has one pass and one fail: entropy 1. So does the moderate group. The excellent group has two passes: entropy 0. Weighted by size — two students in each — the entropy that remains once you know the grade is (1 + 1 + 0) ÷ 3 ≈ 0.667. Now split by revision instead. Everyone who revised passed: entropy 0. Of the three who did not, one passed and two failed: entropy 0.918. Remaining entropy, (0 + 0.918) ÷ 2 ≈ 0.459.

The information gain of a question is the entropy before it minus the entropy after. Grade: 0.918 − 0.667 = 0.252 bits. Revision: 0.918 − 0.459 = 0.459 bits. Revision tells us more, so a decision tree asks about it first. On the branch where the answer is no, uncertainty remains, and there the tree asks about grade. On the branch where the answer is yes, nothing remains to ask.

Chapter 5 · §1

A machine that predicts the next word

The assistants people now use at work are large language models. They are built from the ideas in Chapter 4, scaled up enormously, and trained to do one thing: predict what comes next.

Pause and thinkFinish this sentence: "The technician restarted the …". How many reasonable endings can you think of? What made some more likely than others?

You thought of several endings and ranked them without effort: server before meeting, and certainly before banana. A language model does the same thing with numbers. Text is first cut into tokens — short pieces, often a word or part of a word. Given the tokens so far, the model computes a probability for every possible next token, picks one, appends it, and repeats. A whole answer is produced one token at a time in this way.

The probabilities come from training. A neural network with billions of weights is shown vast amounts of text and adjusted, by the gradient methods behind Chapter 4's single neuron, until its predictions of the next token are good. Nobody writes rules into it. Grammar, facts, styles of argument and a great deal else are absorbed because they help with prediction. Its quality is then judged on text it has not seen — held-out data — because performance on the training text itself proves only memory. And it inherits the slant of what it read: if the training text describes some groups or views more often or more kindly than others, so will the model.

Picking the next token involves a setting called temperature, a direct descendant of the temperature in Chapter 2. At a low temperature the model nearly always takes the most likely token: predictable and repetitive. At a high one it samples more freely among the alternatives: varied, sometimes inventive, sometimes wrong. This is why the same question can produce different answers on different occasions.

Chapter 5 · §2

What the model can see: context

A model answers from two things only: what it absorbed in training, and what is in front of it now. The second is called the context, and managing it is most of the practical skill.

Pause and thinkYou explain a project to an assistant on Monday. On Tuesday, in a new conversation, it knows nothing about it. Has it forgotten?

It never knew, in the sense you mean. The model's weights do not change when you talk to it. Everything it "knows" about your conversation is the text currently in its context window: your instructions, any documents you supplied, and the exchange so far. A new conversation starts with an empty window. Products add continuity by putting things back into the window for you — a Project's instructions and files, a stored memory — but the principle is unchanged: if it is not in the window and was not in the training text, the model cannot use it.

The window is large but finite, and measured in tokens. In English a token is roughly four characters, so a page of text is several hundred tokens. Within the window a mechanism called attention lets the model weigh every token against every other, which is how it connects a question at the end to a clause on page three. Attention is not perfect: in a very full window, material in the middle is used less reliably, and instructions given long ago compete with everything since.

Chapter 5 · §3

Why it gets things wrong, and what helps

A model that produces the likeliest continuation will, when it lacks the facts, produce a likely-sounding one. That single property explains most of its failures and points to the remedies.

Pause and thinkYou ask an assistant for your own company's returns period. It has never seen your policy. What do you expect it to say?

Not "I do not know", as often as you would hope. A confident, fluent, plausible answer is the likeliest continuation of your question, so that is what tends to appear: thirty days, perhaps, because that is common. This is a hallucination — not a malfunction but the ordinary mechanism working without the facts. Three limits produce most of them. The model's knowledge stops at its training cut-off, so recent events are missing. It never saw private information: your policies, customers and figures. And it is poor at exact work such as long arithmetic, because it predicts digits instead of calculating them.

Each limit has a remedy, and they are the same remedies Chapter 6 teaches as habits. Grounding: put the source in the context and ask the model to answer from it; it is far more reliable reading than recalling. Retrieval: let the product find the relevant passages from a larger store and place them in the window for you. Tools: let the model call a search engine for what is recent and a calculator or code for what must be exact. None of these makes checking unnecessary. A grounded answer is only as good as the document it was grounded in, as the last button in the experiment shows.

What does a language model actually compute at each step?
A probability for every possible next token given the tokens so far. One is chosen, appended, and the process repeats.
Why does the same prompt sometimes give different answers?
The next token is sampled from a probability distribution. At temperatures above the minimum, less likely tokens are sometimes chosen, and one different token changes everything after it.
An assistant "forgets" yesterday's conversation. Explain.
Its weights did not change. Yesterday's text is not in today's context window unless a Project, a memory feature or the user puts it there.
Give three causes of hallucination and a remedy for each.
Events after the training cut-off: search. Private information never seen: supply or retrieve the document. Exact calculation: a calculator or code tool. In every case, verify the result.

Chapter 6 · §1

Prompting and task execution

A prompt is a brief. Everything you would tell a capable new colleague before handing over a piece of work belongs in it, and almost every disappointing answer can be traced to something the brief left out.

Pause and thinkYou ask a new member of staff to "write something about our new product". What will they have to guess? Count the guesses. Each one is a place where the result can differ from what you wanted.

They must guess the length, the reader, the purpose, the tone, the facts, the format, and what you would regard as good. An assistant makes the same guesses, silently and plausibly. A complete brief has five parts, and it is worth learning them as a list.

Large jobs: decompose, then iterate

A request for "a market report" asks for research, analysis, structure and prose all at once, and gives you one opportunity to notice a mistake — at the end, when it is expensive. Break it up. Ask first for the outline and agree it. Then the evidence for each section. Then the draft. Then the tightening. Each step is small enough to check, and an error caught at the outline costs a sentence to fix.

The same attitude governs a single answer. A first response is a draft. Say what is right about it, what is wrong, and what to change; do not start again from nothing, and do not accept it because it reads well.

When a requirement is missed, ask why

Suppose the answer ignores something you wanted. Before rewriting the prompt, diagnose it. The requirement was either absent (you never said it), ambiguous (you said it in a way that could be read two ways — Chapter 4), buried (you said it once, in the middle of a long paragraph), or contradicted (you said something else that conflicts with it). The remedy differs in each case: add it, sharpen it, move it to where it cannot be missed, or resolve the conflict.

The Calder caseIn a two-week trial, Calder's agents first used the assistant however they liked. Most typed "reply to this customer" and pasted the ticket. Team leads found that 38% of the drafts needed substantial rework: wrong tone, no reference to policy, a remedy the agent had not chosen. Chapter 6 · §2

Output evaluation and validation

Of the seven skills this one matters most. An assistant's answer arrives well written, well organised and confident whether or not it is right. The whole of this section is about refusing to be persuaded by that.

Pause and thinkYou ask an assistant, "Are you sure that figure is correct?" and it replies, "Yes, I am confident." What have you learned?

Nothing you can rely on. The reply is produced by the same process that produced the figure; it is not a second opinion. This is Chapter 1's lesson in its sharpest form — a model's report of its own confidence is not independent verification — and it is the single point most worth remembering from this chapter. Verification means comparison with something outside the conversation: the source document, a system of record, a calculation you run yourself, a person who knows.

The claim–evidence table

The practical instrument for accuracy is dull and effective. List each factual claim in the draft. Beside it, write where in the source it is supported. Three outcomes are possible: the source supports it; the source is silent, in which case the claim is unsupported, however reasonable it sounds; or the source contradicts it. Only the first kind may stay without further work. Most people, on first trying this, are surprised by how many claims fall into the second group.

Bias, and when to call a person

An answer can be accurate in every sentence and still slanted: one option described warmly and the others briefly, a group characterised by a stereotype, a conclusion that suits whoever asked. Look for what was left out and whether alternatives were treated evenly. And know the limits of your own checking. Where the content is legal, medical, financial, safety-critical or contractual, or where a mistake would be costly and hard to undo, the right validation is review by someone qualified, not a more careful reading by you.

Choosing the form of the output

Evaluation includes deciding what shape the answer should take once it is right. A short answer to be read once belongs inline in the chat. Information to be sorted, compared or passed to another tool belongs as structured data — a table, a list with fixed fields. A substantial piece that will be edited, reused or shared — a report, a plan, a document — belongs in an Artifact, where it sits apart from the conversation and can be revised without retyping.

Worked exampleA colleague sends you an assistant-drafted client update: fluent, three paragraphs, four figures. You run the five checks. Accuracy: two figures match the monthly report, one is not in it, one is last quarter's. Completeness: the client's question about delivery dates is not answered. Consistency: the percentage in paragraph one does not follow from the numbers in paragraph two. Relevance and audience fit are fine. Verdict: not ready. It read perfectly well, which is exactly why the checks are needed.

How much checking is enough?

Checking costs time, and time was the thing you were trying to save. A manager therefore has to decide how much review a workflow gets, and the honest answer depends on what an error costs. Review everything and the saving shrinks; review nothing and the errors go out under your name. Between the two lie sampling and risk-based review, in which the routine case is spot-checked and the consequential case is always read. There is no universally right setting, which is why the next experiment has no right answer.

Chapter 6 · §3

Product and model selection

Claude is not one thing. It is several ways of working and several models, and a good deal of wasted effort comes from using the wrong one — a research question asked in a quick chat, or the most powerful model set to sorting email.

Three model families

The families are named Haiku, Sonnet and Opus, and they trade the same three things against one another: capability, speed and cost. Haiku is the smallest and quickest, suited to high-volume, well-defined work — classifying, extracting, short replies. Sonnet is the middle of the range and the sensible default for most drafting and analysis. Opus is the most capable, for long, subtle or many-step reasoning where a better answer justifies waiting and paying more. There is no best model in general; there is only the smallest one that meets the quality the task requires. Choose by what the task demands, not by a league table.

Chapter 6 · §4

Workflow integration and solution design

Using an assistant for a task is easy. Putting one into a process that other people depend on is design, and this section is about doing that design properly.

Pause and thinkTake a process you know well — answering a customer complaint, say. Which steps are slow because they are laborious, and which are slow because someone must exercise judgement? Would you speed up both in the same way?

You would not, and that distinction is the heart of the matter. Start, as Chapter 1 taught, with the performance measure: what does a good outcome look like, and how will you know? Then map the process as it is. For each step note the delays, the repetitive transformations (copying, reformatting, summarising), the decisions, the approvals, where sensitive data appears, and what happens if the step goes wrong.

Now sort the steps. Some are accelerable: drafting, summarising, classifying, extracting, reformatting. The assistant does these quickly and a person can check the result. Others are judgement-dependent: deciding on a refund, approving an exception, signing off a commitment. These stay with a named person, though the assistant may prepare the material. The aim is not to automate everything; a design that hands a consequential decision to the assistant is a bad design, however much time it saves.

Four things every design must state

ElementMeaning
InputsWhat the assistant is given at each step, and from where — and what it must not be given
Validation gatesThe points at which a person checks the output before it goes further
Decision ownerThe named role accountable for the final decision
Escalation pathWhat happens to the unusual, the uncertain and the high-risk case

Saying what it will and will not do

Part of the design is explaining it to the people who must approve it and live with it. State the expected value in terms they measure — hours saved, turnaround time, consistency. State the limits just as plainly: it can be wrong, it knows only what it is given, it does not replace the reviewer. State the risks and the human involvement the workflow still needs. And know where your own role ends. A request to connect systems, run without a person present, or process things automatically at volume is a job for a developer or architect; recognising that and handing it over is good judgement, not a failure.

Worked exampleA support team spends twenty minutes on each complaint. Mapping shows eight of those minutes go on reading the thread and looking up the order, seven on drafting, and five on deciding the remedy. The design: the assistant summarises the thread and drafts a reply from the agent's chosen remedy (accelerable); the agent decides the remedy and approves the text (judgement, validation gate); anything involving a legal threat, a safety issue or a refund above the agent's limit goes to the team lead (escalation). Expected saving, about ten minutes a ticket; decision ownership unchanged.

Is it worth doing? The business case

A design is not a decision. Someone must still be persuaded to fund it, and that takes four numbers: the benefit, the running cost, the one-off cost, and the time to pay back. Two traps catch nearly every first attempt. The first is assuming that everyone will use the tool; in practice adoption starts well below 100% and has to be earned. The second is counting every hour saved as money saved. An agent who finishes tickets faster has gained capacity, and capacity only becomes value if it is used — to clear the backlog, to answer sooner, to avoid hiring. The share that does is the realisation rate, and it is a management choice, not a property of the software.

Chapter 6 · §5

Configuration and knowledge management

Chapter 1 made the point that an agent can act only on what it can observe. A Project is how you decide, in advance and for every conversation, what Claude can observe.

A Project has two parts, and confusing them is the commonest mistake. Project instructions are standing orders: who the audience is, what tone to take, what format to use, what never to do. They apply to every chat in the Project without being repeated. Project knowledge is reference material: the policy, the price list, the style guide, last year's report. It is what Claude reads, not how Claude behaves.

Pause and thinkYou explain your house style carefully in one chat. Next week you open a new chat in the same Project and the style is gone. Why — and where should the explanation have been put?

Because context is not shared between chats unless it has been placed where every chat can see it. Something said in one conversation stays in that conversation. The style belonged in the Project instructions; a document it relied on belonged in Project knowledge. Remember this one fact — instructions and knowledge persist across a Project's chats, conversation does not — and most of the confusion people have about Projects disappears.

Writing instructions that work

Good standing instructions are short, specific and stable. State the role and audience ("You are helping the support team reply to hosting customers"), the conventions ("British spelling; no promises about dates"), the output shape, and the boundaries ("If a customer mentions legal action, stop and say the case must go to a team lead"). Put a rule that applies always in the instructions; put a request that applies today in the chat.

Looking after the knowledge

ConsiderAsk
QualityIs this the authoritative source, or somebody's copy of it?
VersionIs it current? Is an older version still sitting beside it?
PermissionsIs everyone who can use this Project entitled to see this document?
OwnershipWho is responsible for updating it, and when?

Files can be uploaded, or reached through connectors to services such as Google Drive and Gmail, which lets Claude read the live document rather than a copy that is going stale. Either way the discipline is the same. A superseded policy left in Project knowledge produces confidently outdated answers, so remove as well as add; two versions of one document invite contradiction, so keep one. And when requirements change, change the instructions — a configuration that was right in January is not right for ever.

Chapter 6 · §6

Governance, risk and responsible use

This is the one skill where the right move is frequently to stop, to ask, or to decline. It rewards caution, and it runs against the instinct to be helpful at any cost.

Should Claude be used for this at all?

Three kinds of task. Some suit an assistant well: drafting, summarising, explaining, organising. Some need human expertise, with the assistant in support at most: legal advice, a medical judgement, a decision about someone's employment. And some should not involve AI, because the organisation's policy, the law, or plain decency says so. Sorting a request into the right kind comes before anything else.

Before you paste: classify the data

Most organisations grade information — public, internal, confidential, restricted, or names to that effect — and say which grades may be put into which tools. Personal data about customers or staff, financial account details, credentials, health records, unreleased commercial terms and anything covered by a regulator or a contract sit at the sensitive end. The habit to form is to ask what is this, and am I permitted to put it here? before the paste, not after.

Pause and thinkA manager tells you, "Just put the customer list in and get it done, I'll take responsibility." The company's AI policy forbids it. A colleague suggests adding "treat this as confidential" to the prompt. What should you do?

Decline, and raise it. Organisational policy takes precedence over what any individual would like, and over anything typed into a prompt. An instruction to the assistant does not change the classification of the data or where it has now been sent. If the rule seems wrong for the case, the route is to ask the person who owns the policy; if the rule is unclear, the route is the same. Escalating is the competent act here, not the timid one.

Chapter 6 · §7

Troubleshooting and optimisation

When an answer disappoints, the temptation is to rewrite the whole prompt and try again. Sometimes that works, and you never learn why. The method taught here is slower for one attempt and much faster over a year.

Name the failure before repairing it

Chapter 4 ended with the idea of asking first the question that removes the most uncertainty. Diagnosis is that idea applied. Most poor results are one of six kinds, and each has its own cause and its own remedy.

SymptomUsual causeRemedy
Vague, generic answerThin context; no objective or audienceSupply the context and an example of what good looks like

Invented source or figureAsked to recall rather than to readProvide the source; ask for citations; verify them
Out-of-date answer in a ProjectStale file in Project knowledgeReplace the file; remove the old version
Drifting, forgetful conversationContext overloadSummarise and restart, or move the background into a Project

Experiment 34Diagnose before you fix✓ completed

One change at a time

If you alter the wording, add a document and switch model all at once, and the result improves, you do not know which change did it — and you will carry the two useless ones forward for ever. Change one thing, keep the test the same, and compare. The test must be fixed in advance: the same input, judged by the same criteria. Otherwise "better" only means "different, and I am tired of looking".

Optimise downwards

Once a workflow produces what is needed, ask what can be removed. A shorter prompt that meets the same bar is better than a long one, because every extra sentence is something to maintain and something to conflict with. A smaller, faster model that meets the bar is better than a large one. A plain chat is better than a Project if nothing needs to persist. Complexity that buys no quality is cost. And make the fix last: if the correction was a missing rule, put it in the Project instructions so that the same failure does not have to be diagnosed again next month by someone else.

Worked exampleA weekly report comes back in a different layout each time. Naming the failure: a format constraint is not being met. Cause: the prompt says "a tidy summary", which is ambiguous. One change: replace it with the exact headings and a pasted example of last week's report. Same input, same criteria: the layout now holds for three weeks running. Make it last: move the format into the Project instructions. No change of model was needed, and none was made.
The Calder caseThree months in, the billing queue's reopen rate has risen from 12% to 15% while the other two queues have improved. The temptation is to rewrite the billing prompt, switch model and retrain the team all at once. Calder's team lead instead names the failure — replies that do not resolve the issue — and samples forty reopened tickets. Thirty-one concern refunds to expired cards, a case the billing Project's knowledge does not cover, so the assistant drafts a confident, generic reply. One change is made: a one-page procedure for expired-card refunds is added to Project knowledge. The same measure is watched for four weeks, and the reopen rate returns to 11%. The lesson: the diagnosis came from the data, the fix was a single document, and nobody needed a larger model.
Key ideaName the failure, find its cause, change one thing, test against fixed criteria, then simplify and make the fix permanent. A bigger model is rarely the first answer.
Chapter 6 · §8

From pilot to practice: the manager's view

The seven skills describe a competent individual. An organisation is not improved by competent individuals alone; someone has to decide where to begin, prove that it works, and change how the work is done. No list of personal skills covers that, and it is most of a manager's job.

Pause and thinkCalder's head of service has a sound business case and a willing supplier. She can switch the assistant on for all forty agents on Monday. Why might that be the wrong thing to do, even if the technology works perfectly?

Because a technology that works is not the same as a practice that has changed. On Monday morning forty people would have a new tool, no shared way of using it, no baseline to compare against, and no one to ask. Some would use it badly, some not at all, and in three months nobody could say whether it had helped. The alternative is slower to start and much faster to finish.

Choosing where to start

A good first process isBecause
High in volumeThere is enough work to measure a difference within weeks
Bounded and repeatableThe inputs and the definition of "done" are clear
Low in harm if it failsA mistake is caught by a person and costs little
Already measuredA baseline exists, or can be taken, before you begin
Owned by a willing teamPeople who want it to work will tell you honestly when it does not

At Calder that points to the returns queue: the largest volume, the clearest policy, modest sums at stake. Billing, with its exceptions and its regulated data, is a poor place to learn.

Running the pilot

Fix four things in writing before the first ticket. The success criteria: for example, handling time down by a quarter with error and reopen rates no worse. The comparison: the delivery queue carries on as before. The duration: six weeks, long enough to get past the novelty. And the decision date, with three permitted outcomes — scale, change, or stop. A pilot without a decision date becomes a permanent experiment, costing licences and attention and proving nothing.

The people

Resistance is rarely about the software. Agents ask, reasonably, whether saved time means fewer jobs, whether they will be blamed for the assistant's mistakes, and whether the skilled part of their work is being taken away. Each deserves a straight answer before launch. Say what the saved time is for — at Calder, bringing first response from nine hours to four, not reducing headcount. Say that the agent approves every reply and that a mistake caught in review is the process working. And redesign the role honestly: less typing, more deciding, more of the difficult cases. Then train for the new role and not merely the new tool. An hour on which buttons to press is not training; practice in writing a brief, checking a draft against a source and knowing when to escalate is.

Scaling, and keeping it honest

If the figures support it, extend queue by queue, carrying over what was learned: the prompt template, the review rule, the Project set-up, the checklist for policy changes. Give the whole thing an owner and a regular forum — monthly is enough — that looks at the measures, reviews incidents, and decides what changes. Appoint a few capable agents as the people colleagues ask. And keep measuring after the excitement fades, because a workflow drifts: policies change, staff turn over, files go stale. The organisations that get lasting value treat the assistant as a process to be managed, not a product to be installed.

The same method somewhere else: accounts payable

A method that works in one department has not been learned until it has been used in another. Here is a second, smaller case with different numbers, and the numbers change the design.

Questions for review

Calder saves 8 net minutes on each assisted ticket. Why is the monthly benefit not simply 12,000 × 8 minutes × £24 an hour?
Because not every ticket is assisted (adoption) and not every saved hour becomes value (realisation). At 70% adoption and 50% realisation the labour benefit is 12,000 × 0.7 × 8 ÷ 60 × £24 × 0.5 = £13,440, not £38,400. Licence costs and any change in error cost must then be added.
Why should every speed measure be paired with a quality measure?
Speed can be bought with errors or with replies that do not resolve the problem. Handling time watched alone rewards exactly that; error rate, reopen rate and satisfaction reveal it.
What makes a good pilot?
A bounded process with enough volume to measure, a willing team, low harm if it fails, a baseline recorded beforehand, a comparison group, success criteria agreed in advance, and a fixed date on which to decide: scale, change or stop.
Name the five components of a complete prompt and say what each contributes.
Objective (what is to be produced), context (for whom, why, from what material), constraints (what it must and must not do), output shape (the form of the answer), quality criteria (how both parties know it is good).
Why is asking an assistant "are you sure?" not a form of verification?
The assurance is generated by the same process as the original claim, so it is not independent. Verification requires comparison with something outside the conversation: the source, a system of record, a calculation, or a qualified person.
A team drafts tender responses weekly from the same reference documents. Which feature, and why?
A Project: standing instructions carry the house style, Project knowledge carries the references, and both persist across chats. An Artifact suits each finished response; the model should be the smallest that meets the quality required.
What four things must a workflow design state?
The inputs the assistant receives, the validation gates where a person checks output, the named owner of the final decision, and the escalation path for unusual, uncertain or high-risk cases.
Distinguish Project instructions from Project knowledge.
Instructions are standing rules of behaviour — audience, tone, format, boundaries. Knowledge is reference material to read — policies, price lists, style guides. Both persist across the Project's chats; what is said in one chat does not.
A manager asks you to enter restricted customer data against policy. What do you do?
Decline and escalate to whoever owns the policy. Organisational policy takes precedence over an individual's instruction and over anything written in a prompt; labelling the data "confidential" in the prompt changes nothing.
Describe a disciplined way to fix a prompt that is producing poor output.
Name the failure type; identify the likely cause; change one variable; re-test on the same input against the same criteria; keep the change only if it helps; then simplify, and store a lasting fix in the Project instructions.
